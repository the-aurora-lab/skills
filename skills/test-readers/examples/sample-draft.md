# Fresh Air Classrooms: project summary

## Background

Children spend around a thousand hours a year in classrooms, and many of those rooms are poorly ventilated. In winter, windows stay shut to keep the heat in and CO2 levels routinely climb past 2,000 ppm by the end of a lesson. High CO2 is a marker of stale air, and stale air is linked in the literature to drowsiness, slower reaction times and the spread of respiratory infections. Most schools have no way of knowing when this is happening, because IAQ is invisible and the guidance assumes mechanical ventilation that older buildings simply do not have.

## Our pilot

Last spring we installed low-cost NDIR monitors in three Year 5 classrooms at one primary school for six weeks. Each node shows a traffic-light display and sends readings to a simple dashboard that teachers can check on there phones. When the light turned amber, teachers opened a window for five minutes. Average CO2 fell from 1,850 ppm to 1,050 ppm, and the pilot proved that the dashboards reduce pupil absences by 20%. Teachers told us the devices were easy to use and that the alert did not disrupt lessons, one teacher said it had become part of the classroom routine and the children reminded her when the light changed which shows the approach can work at scale.

## What we will do

Over the next eighteen months we will roll the sensors out to 48 classrooms across 12 primary schools in the Northfield area, chosen to include both Victorian buildings and post-war system builds. Each school will receive a half-day training session and a short guide for teachers. We will compare air quality and ACH estimates before and after installation, and an independent evaluator will look at whether the program effects attendance, using school records for the same classrooms in the previous year as a baseline. Results will be shared with the local authority and with the 10 participating schools through a short report and an open dashboard.

## Why it matters

Upgrading ventilation in an older school typically costs tens of thousands of pounds per building. If a monitor costing under £100 and a simple habit can deliver most of the benefit, the approach could be adopted by any school in the country within a single budget cycle. The project will also produce an open dataset of classroom air quality across building types, which does not currently exist for the region.
